Stars

This exercise will explore stars and their colour, building a small interactive streamlit app.

1 · The colour of a star

Planck’s law, named after Max Planck, gives the spectrum radiated by a black body: an idealised object that absorbs all light falling on it, so everything it emits comes from its own heat:

\[B_\lambda(T)=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\, \frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda k T)}-1}.\]

\(B_\lambda\) is the emitted power per unit wavelength, \(\lambda\) is the wavelength, \(T\) is the temperature in kelvin (the only free variable in the equation), \(h\) is Planck’s constant (which sets the energy carried by a photon of a given wavelength), \(c\) is the speed of light and \(k\) is Boltzmann’s constant (which converts temperature into energy). A star’s surface radiates close to this law, so temperature fixes the shape of a star’s spectrum. The following plots the spectra of stars radiating at four different temperatures:

Hotter surfaces push their peak toward shorter wavelengths. A 3,000 K surface peaks in the infrared, with light entering the visible band from the red end. A 20,000 K surface peaks in the ultraviolet and floods the visible band from the blue end. Real stars cover roughly 2,300 K (the coolest red dwarfs) to 50,000 K (the hottest blue O-type stars). The Sun is 5,772 K, intermediate on a logarithmic temperature scale.

The L class represents brown dwarfs, objects which glow faintly but are too light to ignite hydrogen and become stars.

NoteWhat colour is the sun?

The Sun is catalogued as a yellow dwarf, yet seen from space it is essentially white. It looks yellow from the surface of the Earth because the atmosphere scatters short wavelengths out of the direct beam far more strongly than long ones (this is Rayleigh scattering, the same effect that causes the daytime sky to appear blue). Shifted by this effect toward longer wavelengths, direct sunlight appears yellow. Near the horizon, where the air path is longer, the colour shifts further toward orange-red (sunrise/sunset).

Astronomers sort stars into spectral classes, labelled by letters and ordered hot to cool: O, B, A, F, G, K, M.

class surface temperature traditional name true colour example
O above 30,000 K blue blue-white Alnitak
B 10,000 to 30,000 K blue-white blue-white Rigel
A 7,500 to 10,000 K white “cool” white Sirius
F 6,000 to 7,500 K yellow-white white Procyon
G 5,200 to 6,000 K yellow “warm” white the Sun
K 3,700 to 5,200 K orange pale orange Arcturus
M below 3,700 K red orange Betelgeuse

The Sun belongs to class G. Traditional names are slightly “redder” than the black body actually looks, partly for contrast and partly because the Earth’s atmosphere warms the tint of stars as they appear.

2 · The Planckian locus

Part A introduced the spectral locus: a shape enclosing every visible chromaticity, with pure chromatic wavelengths on the rim. A black body at a given temperature has one spectrum, so it corresponds to a single point on that diagram. Adjusting the black body temperature moves this point over a curve known as the Planckian locus, which traces the previous strip of colours as a path. The slider moves a marker along the curve.

The curve starts deep in the red, passes through orange and white near the Sun’s 5,772 K, and bends into pale blue. Push the slider from 10,000 K up to 40,000 K and the marker barely moves: every very hot star looks a similar shade of blue-white. Almost all the colour variation occurs in the lower ranges of temperature. What does this fact suggest about the scale being used on the temperature slider?

ImportantWhy are stars red and blue, but never green?

A black body radiates across the whole visible band at once. A star whose spectrum peaks in the green (like our Sun’s does), near 500 nm, still creates red and blue light beside it; the eye sums the mixture of wavelengths and perceives white. Cooling tints the mixture red while heating tints it blue. No temperature removes both short and long wavelengths in favour of medium wavelengths, which is what a green-perceived mixture would require.

Nor is there any temperature that would yield the opposite mixture of wavelengths (which would be perceived as purple or pink). The Planckian locus crosses the white centre of the chromaticity diagram and never bends toward the green edge or the line of purples.

As a corrollary, the discovery of an “impossible” star coloured with a hue not found on the Planckian locus — if this ever were to happen — could be plausibly recognised as an extraterrestrial technosignature.

The Planckian locus is also the basis of everyday lighting. A bulb sold as “warm white 2,700 K” matches the black body at 2,700 K; an office tube at 5,000 K sits further along the curve. A screen’s default white point, called D65, sits near 6,500 K. Note the backwards vocabulary. Red-end lighting is called “warm” and blue-end lighting is called “cool” even though this is the reverse of the actual temperatures that generate these tints!

3 · A star catalogue

Between 1989 and 1993 the European Space Agency satellite Hipparcos measured the positions, brightnesses and colours of every star it could see. The page serves the complete catalogue down to visual magnitude 9.5: 98,653 stars, one row each.

The twelve columns: HIP is the star’s number in the Hipparcos catalogue; ecl_lon and ecl_lat place it on the sky in ecliptic coordinates; color_r, color_g, color_b hold a ready-made display colour for the map below (note that these colours are deliberately saturated to be more differentiable, which is why they do not match the shades compared earlier); plx and e_plx are the measured parallax and its standard error, in milliarcseconds; pmra and pmde are the star’s annual drift across the sky, in milliarcseconds per year.

Magnitude

vmag is apparent brightness on the magnitude scale: smaller numbers mean brighter. A dark sky allows people to see stars up to roughly magnitude 6.5 with the naked eye; stars “above” this magnitude (up to a maximum of 9.5) are invisible without a telescope. Dim stars outnumber bright stars; the counts grow fast as the limit increases (roughly tripling with each unit).

We can see this distribution using a histogram. Both panels share the same half-magnitude bins, split in colour at magnitude 6.5, the naked-eye limit. The left panel counts on a linear axis, the right on a logarithmic one.

A density plot is an alternative to the histogram, normalising so the area under the curve equals one, then smoothing the bin edges away by convolving it with a small Gaussian kernel:

The area under any stretch of the distribution curve is the fraction of stars in that magnitude range, and the whole shape encloses exactly one. This holds only on a linear vertical axis. A logarithmic axis would stretch the flat bright-end tail into view, at the cost of distorting the relative proportions as suggested by area.

A related question asks: “how many stars are this bright or brighter”. Summing histogram bins from the left to the right and dividing by the total generates a cumulative distribution. The height of this cumulative distribution for a given magnitude will be the fraction of the catalogue at that magnitude or brighter or brighter (on the backwards magnitude scale “brighter” means a smaller magnitude number).

A cumulative distribution function always starts at zero and climbs to one (i.e. 100%) monotonically.

Note the difference in colour boundaries; in the density plot the boundary is vertical, because a star’s position on the magnitude axis decides which group it belongs to. In the cumulative plot the boundary is horizontal, because the cumulative total past 6.5 includes both kinds (everything stacked above the horizontal boundary arrived from fainter, invisible stars).

Apparent magnitude should not be confused with actual magnitude! The variable vmag records brightness as seen from Earth, which is determined by two factors; how much light the star puts out, and its distance from our solar system (brightness falls with the square of distance). Sirius looks brightest in the night sky at 2.6 parsecs; Rigel looks fainter from a hundred times further away while shining thousands of times harder.

Temperature

bv_color is the B minus V colour index: the star’s magnitude through a blue filter minus its magnitude through a yellow-green one. A hot blue star is brighter in B and gets a smaller or negative index value, while a cool star gets a large value. B minus V is an approximation for surface temperature that runs in reverse (the index falling as the temperature rises).

Spectral classes themselves are defined by neither colour nor temperature. The Morgan-Keenan system classifies a star by the absorption lines in its spectrum, anchored to designated standard stars. Colour and temperature are calibrations laid on top of that: for each class, astronomers measure the mean B minus V of nearby unreddened stars and infer the effective temperature from models. The class boundaries used below are calibrations based on Pecaut and Mamajek (2013).

Class M (red) stars make up roughly seventy percent of the galaxy’s stars, but only a small share of the stars in this catalogue. This is because the vast majority of M stars are red dwarfs that shine so faintly that a brightness-limited catalogue misses nearly all of them. The large population of red dwarf stars suggests a relatonship should exist between star magnitude and star temperature. A scatter plot is the obvious chart type for plotting this relationship. The following plot using the colour index (as a proxy for temperature) as the x-axis, with apparent magnitude the y-axis. 4 · Distance, radius, and mass

As vmag cannot distinguish between a nearby dim star and a distant bright one, we need additional data — specifically distance — to establish the actual relationship between temperature and magnitude.

Hipparcos does not include a distance field, but does include measures of parallax. As Earth circles the Sun, a nearby star appears to shift against the far background. The size of that annual shifts indicate the distance; one arcsecond of parallax defines one parsec (3.26 light years).

Distance in parsecs is 1000 divided by the parallax. The Hipparcos plx is the parallax in milliarcseconds and e_plx its standard error; measurements are often highly imprecise. With a distance measure, apparent magnitude can be converted to absolute magnitude; the brightness a star would show from a standard 10 parsecs:

\[M = m - 5\log_{10}(d / 10\,\mathrm{pc}).\]

Rather than size, absolute magnitude is a measure of luminosity; the star’s total power output. A stars luminosity is a function of its surface area and its surface temperature, related by the the Stefan-Boltzmann law. Dividing the luminosity (total power) by the power emitted per square metre at its temperature yields an estimate of the star’s surface area, from which its radius can be estimated.

Smaller high-mass stars can be hotter and brighter than larger low-mass stars. Radius indicates size, but not mass. An approximate relationship has been calibrated between luminosity and mass based on measurements of the orbital periods of binary star systems. For regular stars, luminosity is estimated to increase roughly as mass to the power 3.5. A star’s temperature and luminosity can thereby furnish estimates of its mass and radius. These are the axes used by the Triangle of Everything visualisation from the previous session.

5 · The Main Sequence

The Hipparcos star catalogue was merged with two others — Yale and Gliese — in the HYG catalogue (the name an acronym of its sources). HYG comes with pre-calculated and pre-cleaned columns for distance and absolute magnitude; respectively dist and absmag.

The columns: proper is the star’s traditional name, which only a few thousand stars have, so the cell above filters to named rows (the unfiltered table is mostly blank there). dist is the distance in parsecs (the Sun’s is zero). mag is apparent magnitude (the same scale as vmag in the Hipparcos table), and absmag is the absolute magnitude computed from mag and dist by the formula above. ci is the B minus V colour index (the same quantity as bv_color). Can you guess what con is?

We will use this data set to generate the canonical chart of stellar astronomy, the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram of absolute magnitude against colour/temperature. The chart will colour each star by its physical hue. As HYG stores no display colours, these will be derived from the ci (B minus V) index variable. This variable can be converted to a temperature through an empirical black-body formula fitted by Ballesteros (2012):

\[T = 4600\left(\frac{1}{0.92\,(B{-}V) + 1.7} + \frac{1}{0.92\,(B{-}V) + 0.62}\right)\ \mathrm{K}.\]

This temperature is then converted to a colour through bb_rgb as discussed above. Converting 107,860 stars one at a time would be slow, so the cell rounds the B minus V range into 48 narrow bins, giving every star a common bin colour. This produces a smooth gradient unlike the discrete visible colour-bands revealed by the Hipparcos saturated RGB values.

The diagram shows a strong correlation between temperature and absolute magnitude driven by the diagonal band that runs from the upper left to the lower right: hotter stars shine brighter. This diagonal band is the main sequence: the population of “normal” stars (including the Sun) that fuse hydrogen in their cores.

NoteThe HR Diagram

The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram is over a century old and has become one of the most recognisable scientific visualisations. Run a Google image search for it and browse the variants: axes in temperature, colour index or spectral class; luminosity or absolute magnitude; point clouds, density shading, evolutionary tracks. Consider the strengths and weaknesses of these design decisions.

Notice one strange feature shared by nearly all of them. The temperature-brightness relationship is positive, yet the main sequence slopes downward, because the temperature axis runs backwards! The measured colour index increases as stars get cooler, so hot sits on the left and cool on the right. This is a relic; the first such diagrams — drawn before colour was understood as temperature — ordered stars by their spectral class letters. Even after the x axis became understood as temperature, the original orientation has remained as a convention.

Stellar lifecycle

Stars generally spend about ninety percent of their lifetime as main sequence stars, with their position along the band is essentially determined by its mass at “birth”; more massive stars are hotter, brighter; less massive stars are cooler and dimmer. This also determines the length of their lifetimes as stars, with the hottest stars burning through their hydrogen orders of magnitude faster than the coolest stars.

A star begins its “death” when hydrogen in the core is depleted. The spent core contracts while the surface cools and the outer layers swell enormously, becoming brighter and redder. On the HR diagram, star moves from the the main sequence diagonal into the distinct group of lower-temperature giants in the upper right. Sun-like stars eventually lose these outer layers, becoming a small white dwarf (these appear in the lower left of the HR diagram).

More massive stars undergo core collapse and explode in a supernova, leaving behind a neutron star or a black hole. Neither of these appear on the HR diagram — neutron stars are too dim, and black holes emit no light at all — however they do feature in the Triangle of Everything, where black holes are represented by the Schwarzchild wall.

In the case of those main sequence stars for which mass can be reliably estimated from luminosity, radius can be reliably estimated from mass using an exponent of 0.8. The exponent is relatively close to one since radius needs to grow with mass to maintain an equilibrium between the inward gravity pressure and outward radiation pressure. The exponent’s difference from one is reflected in the difference between the slope formed by the main sequence stars in mass-radius space and the lines of constant density.

6 · Age

A simple model defines a star’s life stage by its mass at birth and its current age. Mass fixes the main-sequence properties of luminosity and radius. Mass also determines how quickly a star will form; for mass \(M\), contraction time is approximately \(0.03\,M^{-1.5}\) billion years. Before the contraction time, the star is a protostar.

A star will remain on the main sequence from contraction until approximately \(10\,M^{-2.5}\) billion years, with a floor of about three million years. Below about a third of a sun a second correction stretches the other end: such stars are fully convective, mix all their hydrogen through the core, and burn nearly the whole supply instead of the central tenth, roughly doubling the lifetime by a tenth of a sun. Up to fifteen percent past that lifetime, it will be giant (for stars above eight suns, a supergiant, blue first and red later), after which it will become a remnant. Mass also determines the type of remnant a star will become (white dwarf, neutron star, or black hole).

A chart showing these boundaries is called a phase plane (or phase diagram). Axes are the model’s numeric inputs — in this case mass and age — and the plane is divided into regions where each region represents a categorical output — in this case, the stage of star life.

The main-sequence lifetime runs from a few million years at sixty suns to more than a trillion years at a fifth of a sun, nearly a million times longer. The age of the universe is usually estimated around 13.7 billion years, which means that even the oldest red dwarf today is still fairly close to the beginning of its life; no red dwarf has yet had anywhere near enough time to burn out. On linear axes every boundary would lie crushed against the edges; a logarithmic turns the power laws into straight lines whose slopes are the exponents (minus 1.5 and minus 2.5).

NoteWhen will the last star die?

The universe is currently in its starry Stelliferous Era, but is believed to have long passed its “cosmic noon” or period of peak star formation. Physics predicts that all stars will eventually die without new ones being formed, leaving behind a dark, cold “ghost universe” populated only by white dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes.

Does the phase plane give you enough information to estimate how far into the future this will be?

(If this question interests you, watch Timelapse of the Future: a Journey to the End of Time.)

Stars formed from the collision of smaller stars can reach an extremely high mass not attainable by naturally forming stars. These stars’ luminosity is near the Eddington limit, causing them to burn out on a similar schedule of roughly three million years.

7 · Metallicity

The two-variable model with age and mass is a simplification. A third variable, metallicity, also affects a star’s lifecycle. This is the mass fraction of a star made of anything heavier than helium (astronomers call all such elements metals, including non-metal elements like carbon and oxygen). The Sun’s metallicity is about 0.02.

The Big Bang produced almost pure hydrogen and helium, so the first stars formed with essentially no metals. The elements heavier than helium were forged by the nuclear fusion inside stars, which builds heavier elements up from lighter ones, in a process that ends with iron (atomic numnber 26). Heavier elements, such as gold, are only created in the supernova explosions of stars much heavier than our sun.

The first generation of stars scattered “metals” into the interstellar gases that would later condense and ignite into the next generation of stars, now able to contain elements other than just hydrogen and helium. Later population of stars formed from richer gases would have higher metalliciites. At 0.02, the Sun (a later population star) is metal rich compared to older stars.

Metallicity affects luminosity. \(L = M^{3.5}\, z^{-0.1}\), where \(z = Z / 0.02\) for metallicity relative to the Sun. This means that metal-poor stars are slightly brighter and hotter at the same mass.

Metallicity also affects the remnant boundary; metal-rich stars shed more of their mass in winds before dying, meaning that a higher birth mass is needed for the star’s supernova to leave a black hole. The model puts the boundary at \(18 + 7z\) suns; eighteen for metal-poor stars rising to twenty-five for stars with the Sun’s metallicity.

The phase plane drawn in the previous section implicitly uses the sun’s metallicity of 0.2 for luminosity and the remnant boundary. Allowing metallicity to fall below 0.001 introduces a pair-instability window: a star with mass between 140 and 260 suns and near-zero metallicity will blow itself apart completely, leaving no remnant.

The phase plane below adds metallicity as a slider, and every boundary the model ties to metallicity moves with it. Metal-poor stars are brighter at the same mass, so they burn out sooner: all the lifetime boundaries slide down as \(z^{0.1}\), and the hotter surfaces shift the spectral-class stripes as well. On the remnant side, the neutron-star region widens as metallicity falls, and below 0.001 the pair-instability window opens: a hole in the black-hole region where nothing at all remains.
