Embeddings

This exercise introduces embedding vectors, as well as lower-dimensional projections of higher-dimensional spaces.

1 · RGB as a 3D spectrum embedding

Part A compressed each spectrum, a curve with hundreds of values, into three cone responses. Coloured lights with similar combinations of red, green and blue values look alike. That RGB structure is essentially an embedding vector. An embedding assigns each object a short vector, arranged so that distance carries similarity. The eye embeds spectra in three dimensions.

A similar move works for many kinds of object: songs, faces, products, documents — albeit with vectors much longer than three numbers. This page builds an embedding for words and uses it to draw visualisations of meaning.

2 · Word vectors

Words that appear in the same contexts tend to mean related things: tea and coffee both follow “a cup of”. To measure how similar words are, we compare how often they appear around similar sets of other words. In the below example, we cosider four target words and a “corpus” of six sentences, counting how often they appear near various context words.

Each row is a vector that represents a coordinate in a six-dimensional space. Words with related meanings are likely to have similar rows; tea and coffee get identical rows because they appear with the same companions; dog and cat match each other and share nothing with tea and coffee. Real methods do the same counting over billions of words. At that scale the table becomes enormous: one row and one column for every word in the vocabulary, and almost all zeros, because most pairs of words never appear near each other. These long, sparse rows must be condensed into vectors that retain the pattern of companions.

Two classic methods (predating contemporary Large Language Models) shrink these vectors in different ways. word2vec (Mikolov and colleagues, 2013) trains a small neural network to predict each word from its neighbours, using the trained network’s weights as the vectors. GloVe (Pennington, Socher and Manning, 2014) fits vectors so that the dot product of any two reproduces how often those two words appear together, using counts taken from 6 billion words of Wikipedia and newswire. In either case, the result is fixed vector for each word that is short and dense relative to the size of the vocabulary.

3 · Visualising 50D space

Fifty dimensional space is a challenge to visualise. A first attempt draws each vector as a row of coloured cells:

Each row works like a barcode, and related rows share some stripes. The eye still has to weigh fifty columns at once to judge distance. A similarity matrix condenses each comparison to one number; the angle (cosine) between each pair of vectors:

The matrix shows every pairwise similarity. Three bright blocks appear: animals are close to animals, royals to, capitals to capitals.

To visualise the coordinates that these vectors represent, the dimensionality of the space must be reduced from fifty to two. Principal component analysis (PCA) finds the direction in 50D space with the greatest spread between coordinates, then the direction at right angles with the next greatest spread, and uses those two as the axes of a flat map. The result is a “shadow” of the original space that preserves as much spread as any flat picture can.

The approximation to distance is imperfect; two points far apart in the full space can land close together in the lower-dimensional projection. The projection nevertheless groups representations of words that are semantically similar, as can be seen in the above plot of 358 GloVe vectors.

Tipt-SNE: t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding

Word embedding visualisaitons are often made with t-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008) rather than PCA. While PCA projects the cloud onto two fixed axes (giving a straight-line view of the real space), t-SNE instead rearranges the points from scratch, placing each one near its neighbours from the full space. The rearrangement produces tight clusters that reveal neighbourhood structure in very large point sets, but scrambles the directions that carry relational meaning. The method is stochastic, with each run drawing a different map.

4 · The royal parallelogram

The four words of the analogy sit at the corners of a parallelogram. One pair of sides joins man to woman and king to queen: the gender direction. The other pair joins man to king and woman to queen: the status direction. king − man + woman lands on queen because walking two sides of a parallelogram reaches the opposite corner.

The chart is drawn in PCA space, not in the raw coordinates. The four 50-dimensional vectors are projected to the two directions of greatest spread, exactly as in the previous section. PCA is a linear projection, so a parallelogram in the full space is still a parallelogram in the picture; only its proportions change.

6 · Transformers

word2vec and GloVe date from the years before large language models. Embeddings in contemporary systems (such that those used by ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, etc) are different. These embeddings run to thousands of dimensions and shift with context. Large language models are built on the transformer architecture, introduced in 2017 (the T in GPT stands for Transformer).

A transformer takes in a sequence of word vectors like the ones on this page and passes them through layers that repeatedly reshape them according to surrounding context. The solves the problems caused by each word being assigned a fixed vector that cannot differentiate different meanings (e.g. orange the fruit and orange the colour). A transformer starts from vectors like these and lets the sentence reshape them. The attention mechanism turns words’ similarity scores into weights that sum to one, and adds the weighted mixture of their vectors into its own.
