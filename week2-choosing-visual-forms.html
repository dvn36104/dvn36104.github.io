Choosing Visual Forms

Week 2 · Run, verify, intervene — live in your browser

1 · The yield-curve inversion: deviation

The 10-year minus 2-year Treasury spread is a signed difference read against a meaningful zero: positive is the normal upward slope, negative is an inversion — the classic recession signal. The visual task is deviation: variation above and below a fixed reference. The data is a FRED snapshot (12 August 2026); the values are real observations.

Run the reconstruction:

Verify before you trust it:

Your intervention

Remove the zero line and both fills, leaving a plain line. What can a reader no longer do at a glance? Then recompute the chart with monthly means instead of daily values. Which features survive the smoothing, and does the deviation reading change?

Hint 1 — For monthly means, resample on the date column: spread.set_index("date")["spread_pct"].resample("MS").mean().

Hint 2 — A plain line is just ax.plot(monthly.index, monthly.values). The point of the exercise is what you leave out: no axhline, no fill_between. Compare: can you still spot the inversions instantly?

Fully worked solution:

monthly = spread.set_index("date")["spread_pct"].resample("MS").mean()

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 4.6))
ax.plot(monthly.index, monthly.values, color="#171717", lw=1)
ax.set_xlabel("year")
ax.set_ylabel("10-year − 2-year spread (pp)")
ax.set_title("Plain monthly line: the zero reference is now in your head, not on the page")
plt.show()

Without the zero line and fills, the reader must locate zero on the axis and judge sign themselves — the at-a-glance “inverted or not” reading is gone. Monthly smoothing preserves the broad inversion episodes but erases brief crossings; the deviation reading survives only if the zero reference is drawn.

3 · The Mohs hardness scale: ranking

Friedrich Mohs published his ten-mineral scratch scale in 1812 as a ranked list, not a chart: each mineral scratches every mineral below it. Rank is an ordinal reading — the order is real, the spacing is not. The absolute values below are Tabor’s measured Vickers indentation hardness numbers for the Mohs minerals (Tabor 1954), so both columns are real.

Your intervention

Plot Tabor’s Vickers hardness for the same ten minerals on a common linear scale, then on a log scale. What does the equal-step Mohs presentation conceal? Sort the minerals alphabetically and state which reading survives that order.

Hint 1 — ax.barh(mohs["mineral"], mohs["vickers"]) gives the linear version; ax.set_xscale("log") turns the second panel logarithmic.

Hint 2 — Look at diamond on the linear panel. Its Vickers value is nearly five times corundum’s — a fact the equal steps of the Mohs scale (9 → 10) completely hide.

Fully worked solution:

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 5), sharey=True)
ax1.barh(mohs["mineral"], mohs["vickers"], color="#5b8a72")
ax1.set_xlabel("Vickers hardness (linear)")
ax1.set_title("Linear: diamond dwarfs everything")
ax2.barh(mohs["mineral"], mohs["vickers"], color="#5b8a72")
ax2.set_xscale("log")
ax2.set_xlabel("Vickers hardness (log)")
ax2.set_title("Log: the ratios between steps")
plt.show()

The Mohs presentation conceals magnitude: the step from corundum to diamond is nearly a factor of five, yet it looks identical to every other step. Alphabetical order destroys the ranking reading entirely — only the magnitude reading (bar length) survives a meaningless sort order.

7 · Document-topic mixtures: part-to-whole

A topic model represents each document as a mixture of topics. Every row below is one complete document and its topic proportions sum to 100%. The values are illustrative model output. The part-to-whole task is direct: compare composition within documents and compare those compositions across documents.

Your intervention

Redraw d1 as a pie chart. Which form makes the composition of that one document easier to see? Which form makes comparison across all six documents easier? Then sort the documents by climate share and state which comparison the new order supports.

Hint 1 — ax.pie(d1, labels=topics, colors=topic_colours, autopct="%.0f%%") draws the pie. For the sorted bars, use topic_mix.sort_values("climate", ascending=False) and rebuild the stack.

Fully worked solution:

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 5))

ax1.pie(d1, labels=topics, colors=topic_colours, autopct="%.0f%%")
ax1.set_title("d1 alone: the pie reads fine")

ordered = topic_mix.sort_values("climate", ascending=False)
bottom = np.zeros(len(ordered))
for topic, colour in zip(topics, topic_colours):
    ax2.bar(ordered["document"], ordered[topic], bottom=bottom,
            label=topic, color=colour)
    bottom += ordered[topic].to_numpy()
ax2.set_ylim(0, 1)
ax2.set_title("Sorted by climate share")
ax2.legend(frameon=False, ncol=2, fontsize=8)
plt.show()

For one document the pie is serviceable — a single composition, few slices. For comparison across documents the stacked bars win: six pies would force angle-to-angle comparison, the weakest visual judgement. Sorting by climate share supports exactly one comparison — ranking documents by climate content — and the anchored bottom segment (climate) is the only one that stays easy to read.
