The Triangle of Everything

In 2023, two astrophysicists published a chart that placed every object in the Universe on one pair of axes: quarks, viruses, fleas, whales, planets, stars, black holes, galaxies, and the observable Universe itself (Lineweaver & Patel, All objects and some questions, American Journal of Physics 91:819). In this part you will rebuild that chart, step by step.

1 · One pair of axes for everything

What would a graph of everything compare? Mass and radius are the two properties every physical object has. But from a neutrino to the observable Universe, mass spans about 90 orders of magnitude and radius about 44. This means both axes must be logarithmic.

ImportantApproximate Magnitude

A 70 kg human has a volume of roughly 70 litres because the human body is close to the density of water. The calculation gives 524 litres only because we forced the 50 cm characteristic size into a formula for a sphere. The result does not show that humans have a density of 0.13 g/cm³.

This is a warning about the axis. Radius is a geometric measurement for a planet or star, but an approximate characteristic size for an irregular object such as a flea, human or whale. The chart is designed for comparisons across many orders of magnitude, not precise estimates of each object’s density.

2 · All 29 objects

Now all 29 objects, coloured by kind. Six groups: particles (grey), life, worlds, stars, black holes (black, naturally), and cosmic structures.

The interesting thing is where the points aren’t: Nothing in the top-left (small but extremely heavy), nothing in the bottom-right (huge but almost massless). Two whole corners of possibility, empty. Why?

3 · Lines of constant density

Any object of roughly uniform density \(\rho\) obeys \(m = \frac{4}{3}\pi \rho\, r^3\). Take logs and this becomes \(\log m = 3\log r + \text{const}\): on our axes, a straight line of slope 3, one line per density. Chemistry pins atomic matter near \(\rho \approx 1\ \text{g/cm}^3\); nuclear matter sits fourteen decades higher:

Suddenly the scatter has a skeleton. Everything made of atoms (viruses, fleas, whales, asteroids, planets, the Sun) strings out along the atomic line: that was the straight line hiding in the five familiar objects. The proton and the neutron star, nineteen decades apart in size, share the nuclear line: a neutron star really is matter at the density of an atomic nucleus, the size of a city.

Read that last line again: the least dense thing in the Universe is the Universe, about \(10^{-29}\ \text{g/cm}^3\), six protons per cubic metre.

The top of the table belongs entirely to particles: the top quark and Higgs boson are the densest “objects” plotted, though for them density is a strange notion (their radius is a quantum limit, not a surface). The red giant and white dwarf differ by roughly 12 decades of density despite nearly identical masses; they are the same kind of star at two different moments of its life, which is why mass alone tells you so little and the mass–radius pair tells you so much.

4 · The two physical limits

Now the physics that gives the chart its shape. Both walls are one-line formulas.

The gravity wall. Squeeze a mass \(m\) into a small enough radius and it becomes a black hole. That radius is the Schwarzschild radius \(r_s = 2Gm/c^2\); for a given radius, the heaviest possible object is \(m = rc^2/2G\): slope +1 on our axes. Nothing can sit above this line: an object of that mass would already have collapsed. Black holes sit exactly on it; that is what a black hole is.

The quantum wall. Quantum mechanics denies small masses a definite tiny size: a particle of mass \(m\) cannot be localised below its Compton wavelength \(\lambda_c = \hbar / mc\), so the lightest possible object of radius \(r\) is \(m = \hbar/rc\): slope −1. Below this line, “object” stops meaning anything; try to confine a particle tighter and you create new particles instead. Fundamental particles (top quark, Higgs, electron, neutrino) are plotted on their Compton wavelengths; the composite proton gets its measured charge radius.

The empty corners of Step 2 are now explained, and shaded. They are not unexplored; they are impossible. This is the deepest trick in the figure: the absence of data is carrying the physics, and the shading turns absence into a statement.

One object deserves a second look. Check the top-right corner:

The Universe’s mass and radius satisfy the black-hole relation almost exactly. Does that mean we live inside a black hole? The paper’s answer is careful: the Schwarzschild formula assumes empty space outside the object, and outside our Hubble volume the density carries on much the same, so no. But why the numbers land on the line so precisely is the second exercise.

5 · The Planck scale, and the finished chart

Walls of slope +1 and −1 must meet. Set them equal:

\[\frac{rc^2}{2G} = \frac{\hbar}{rc} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; r = \sqrt{\frac{2G\hbar}{c^3}},\qquad m = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar c}{2G}}.\]

The crossing point is the Planck scale: about \(10^{-33}\) cm and \(2\times10^{-5}\) g, the mass of a flea’s egg compressed to a point \(10^{20}\) times smaller than a proton. The smallest possible object is a Planck-mass black hole, which the paper calls an instanton: the only thing that is simultaneously as small as gravity allows and as small as quantum mechanics allows. Left of the Planck length, physics has no tested theory at all; the paper shades it “sub-Planckian unknown”, and so will we.

Your turn: put the Universe on the wall

The Universe’s point was given to you in the table. Derive it. All you need is the Hubble constant. The radius of the observable Universe is the Hubble radius \(r_H = c/H_0\); its average density is the critical density \(\rho_c = 3H_0^2 / 8\pi G\); and its mass is that density times the volume of the Hubble sphere. Then test whether your derived Universe lands on the black-hole line.

Hint 1: All three formulas are written in the task text above; your job is to translate them into code, in order, using the constants provided. Watch the parentheses in the critical-density formula.

Hint 2: If your ratio comes out wildly far from 1 (say \(10^{-57}\)), you almost certainly forgot to cube \(r_H\) in the volume of the sphere.

How to know you got it. No solution button; the derivation is yours. Done right, the ratio comes out suspiciously exact. That exactness is itself the final puzzle: take the three formulas you just coded, substitute them into each other on paper, and watch what happens to the constants. What you find is a lesson about reading charts, not just making them: when a data point lands perfectly on a theory line, always ask whether the two were secretly computed from the same ingredients.

What went into the chart

Count the ingredients you actually used:

  • one curated table: 29 rows, every value an order-of-magnitude sketch, sources mixed from measurement (Earth), convention (the 50-cm spherical human), and definition (black-hole radii);
  • two log axes: doing more work than everything else combined, turning 90 orders of magnitude into a readable square and power laws into straight lines;
  • one fact about spheres: \(m \propto \rho r^3\), which organises all atomic matter onto one line and all nuclear matter onto another;
  • two formulas: \(r_s = 2Gm/c^2\) and \(\lambda_c = \hbar/mc\), drawn not as data but as walls, turning empty space into a claim;
  • annotation: region labels, line labels, direct point labels: the difference between a scatter plot and an explanation.

That is the whole machine. The famous figure is not famous for its data (half its rows you could have written down yourself) but for its framing: pick axes everything shares, let physics draw the boundaries, and the emptiness becomes the story.
