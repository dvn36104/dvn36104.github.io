The Triangle of Everything

In 2023, two astrophysicists published a chart that placed every object in the Universe on one pair of axes: quarks, viruses, fleas, whales, planets, stars, black holes, galaxies, and the observable Universe itself (Lineweaver & Patel, All objects and some questions, American Journal of Physics 91:819). In this part you will rebuild that chart, step by step.

1 · Everything in the Universe

What would a graph of everything compare? Mass and radius are the two properties every physical object has. But from a neutrino to the observable Universe, mass spans about 90 orders of magnitude and radius about 44. This means both axes must be logarithmic.

Radius is a geometric measurement for a planet or star, but an approximate characteristic size for an irregular object such as a flea, human or whale. The chart is designed for comparisons across many orders of magnitude, not precise estimates of each object’s density.

3 · Lines of constant density

Density is mass divided by volume:

\[\rho = \frac{m}{V}.\]

Here \(\rho\) (the Greek letter rho) is density in grams per cubic centimetre, \(m\) is mass in grams, and \(V\) is volume in cubic centimetres. Rearranging the definition gives \(m = \rho V\) (an object’s mass is its density multiplied by the space it occupies).

For a sphere of radius \(r\), the volume is

\[V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3,\]

where \(r\) is the radius in centimetres and \(\pi\) is the geometric constant pi. Substituting the sphere’s volume into \(m = \rho V\) gives

\[m = \frac{4}{3}\pi \rho r^3.\]

This is exact for a uniform sphere and an approximation when an irregular object is represented by a characteristic radius and an average density.

At a fixed density, doubling the radius multiplies the volume—and therefore the mass—by \(2^3 = 8\).

Taking logs gives \(\log m = 3\log r + \text{constant}\). A constant-density relationship therefore appears as a straight line with slope 3 on the log–log chart.

Different densities have the same slope but different vertical positions.

Atomic density describes typical condensed atomic matter: water, flesh, rock and many solids and liquids often has an average density near \(\rho \approx 1\ \text{g/cm}^3\). Its mass is spread over the full volume occupied by atoms, including the space between nucleus and electrons. Several familiar condensed objects —– viruses, animals, asteroids, planets and the Sun —– fall near the atomic-density line.

Nuclear density is about \(2.3 \times 10^{14}\ \text{g/cm}^3\): fourteen orders of magnitude greater. At this density protons and neutrons are packed together as they are inside an atomic nucleus, so atoms no longer exist as separate structures. One cubic centimetre would contain roughly 230 billion kilograms of matter. Atomic nuclei and neutron stars lie near this density.

Most objects lie between the two lines. However, red giants, galaxy clusters and the Universe have average densities far below the atomic line.

The proton and the neutron star both sit near the nuclear-density line. A neutron star is matter at approximately the density of an atomic nucleus, compressed into an object the size of a city.

The least dense thing in the Universe is (obviously?) the Universe itself, about \(10^{-29}\ \text{g/cm}^3\), six protons per cubic metre.

4 · Two walls

There are no objects in the top-left (small but extremely heavy), or in the bottom-right (huge but almost massless).

This is because physics bounds the set of feasible mass-radius pairings.

The bounds form two “walls”:

The gravity wall. A mass \(m\) becomes a black hole if compressed into a small enough radius; the Schwarzschild radius \(r_s = 2Gm/c^2\). For a given radius, the heaviest possible object is \(m = rc^2/2G\): slope +1 on our axes. Black holes sit exactly on this line, and nothing can go above it.

The quantum wall. According to quantum mechanics, a particle of mass \(m\) cannot be localised below its Compton wavelength \(\lambda_c = \hbar / mc\), so the lightest possible object of radius \(r\) is \(m = \hbar/rc\): slope −1. Below this line, “object” stops meaning anything. Fundamental particles (top quark, Higgs, electron, neutrino) are plotted on their Compton wavelengths.

NoteWhy are the slopes +1 and −1?

On a log–log chart, define

\[x = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{r}{1\ \mathrm{cm}}\right), \qquad y = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{m}{1\ \mathrm{g}}\right).\]

The gravity wall has the form \(m=(c^2/2G)r\). Taking the logarithm turns multiplication into addition. Using the numerical values of the constants in grams and centimetres:

\[y = x + \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{c^2}{2G}\right) = x + 27.83.\]

The coefficient of \(x\) is +1, so increasing radius by one order of magnitude increases the limiting mass by one order of magnitude. The intercept is 27.83: when \(r=1\) cm and therefore \(x=0\), the line gives \(m=6.73\times10^{27}\) g.

The quantum wall has the form \(m=(\hbar/c)r^{-1}\). Taking the logarithm gives

\[y = -x + \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\hbar}{c}\right) = -x - 37.45.\]

The coefficient of \(x\) is −1, so increasing radius by one order of magnitude decreases the limiting mass by one order of magnitude. Its intercept is −37.45: at \(r=1\) cm, the line gives \(m=3.52\times10^{-38}\) g.

The slopes express the exponents of \(r\) and do not depend on the chosen units. The intercepts come from \(G\), \(c\) and \(\hbar\) and do shift if the axes use different units.

The empty corners of Step 2 are now explained and shaded. They are empty because they are impossible.

The absence of data carries a claim from physics. The claim is encoded by the shading.

5 · The Planck scale

The walls of slope +1 and −1 meet at a crossing point:

\[\frac{rc^2}{2G} = \frac{\hbar}{rc} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; r = \sqrt{\frac{2G\hbar}{c^3}},\qquad m = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar c}{2G}}.\]

The crossing point is the Planck scale: about \(10^{-33}\) cm and \(2\times10^{-5}\) g. The smallest possible object is a Planck-mass black hole, which the paper calls an instanton: the only thing that is simultaneously as small as gravity allows and as small as quantum mechanics allows.

Left of the Planck length, physics has no tested theory at all (the paper shades it “sub-Planckian unknown”).
