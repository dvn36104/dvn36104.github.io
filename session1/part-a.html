Allometry

Session 1 · Part A — scaling relationships across levels of aggregation

Allometry is the study of how traits scale with size. Across birds, mammals and reptiles, most size-related traits follow power laws — straight lines once both axes are logged. In this part you will draw the same allometric relationship at three levels of aggregation — individual specimens, species, and families — and watch what aggregation does to the picture.

Two real datasets, served with this page:

Dataset What one row is Rows
AVONET (Tobias et al. 2022, Ecology Letters) one measured individual bird 90,371
Amniote life-history database (Myhrvold et al. 2015, Ecology) one species (birds, mammals, reptiles) 21,322

All code runs in your browser. The first cell may take a few seconds while Python loads.

1 · AVONET: one row per bird

AVONET publishes the raw measurements behind its species table: museum specimens and field captures, measured to a common protocol. We serve four of the original 26 columns — species (BirdLife taxonomy), beak length along the culmen, tarsus length, and wing length, all in millimetres.

2 · Cleaning: where do the rows go?

We want to plot beak length against tarsus length, which means every row we use needs both measurements (and a species name). Museum specimens are imperfect: a damaged bill or a folded skin means one trait was measured and the other was not. Count the gaps before touching anything:

Every column has holes, and they are not the same holes. Keep only the rows that can support the comparison we actually want to make:

We just discarded nearly a quarter of the dataset. That is a legitimate cleaning step — but it is a decision, it changes what “n” means in every caption downstream, and it must be disclosed. Any figure built from this table should say 69,509 individuals, not 90,371.

3 · The individual level

Each point is one bird. Both axes are logged: allometry is about ratios, and a hummingbird and an ostrich need to share a readable axis.

4 · The groupby: from individuals to species

One line of code changes what a point means. groupby("species").mean() collapses every bird of a species into a single average bird:

Averaging removed 59,000 points but the cloud barely tightened (r ≈ 0.61 → 0.62). Within-species measurement scatter is small compared to the real, ecological differences in beak shape between species — hummingbirds, finches and curlews genuinely sit in different places. Aggregation is not a magic de-noiser: it removes exactly the variation that happens within groups, no more.

Your turn: a different trait pair

Repeat the whole pipeline — cleaning included — for wing length vs tarsus length. Wing length has its own, different pattern of missing values, so start from birds, not from clean. Print n after cleaning, and show the species-level n in the plot title.

Hint 1 — The subset to require is ["species", "wing_length_mm", "tarsus_length_mm"]. Beak gaps are irrelevant to this comparison — don’t drop rows for a column you aren’t using.

Hint 2 — birds.dropna(subset=...) then .groupby("species")[["wing_length_mm", "tarsus_length_mm"]].mean(), then ax.scatter(np.log10(...), np.log10(...), s=4, alpha=0.25).

Fully worked solution:

clean_w = birds.dropna(subset=["species", "wing_length_mm", "tarsus_length_mm"])
print(f"{len(birds):,} rows → {len(clean_w):,} rows")

species_w = (clean_w
             .groupby("species")[["wing_length_mm", "tarsus_length_mm"]]
             .mean())

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 6))
ax.scatter(np.log10(species_w["tarsus_length_mm"]),
           np.log10(species_w["wing_length_mm"]),
           s=4, alpha=0.25, color="#e66852")
ax.set_xlabel("log10 tarsus length (mm)")
ax.set_ylabel("log10 wing length (mm)")
ax.set_title(f"Species means (n = {len(species_w):,})")
plt.show()

Wing length is missing in only ~1,100 rows, so this cleaning keeps far more data than the beak comparison did — the n you report depends on the comparison you chose. The wing–tarsus cloud is also tighter than beak–tarsus: both are limb-skeleton traits that track overall body size, while beaks track feeding ecology.

And look at the bottom of your chart: five points stranded far below the cloud, at log10(wing) ≈ −1. Those are the five kiwi species — flightless birds whose wing length is coded as 0.1 mm, a placeholder, not a measurement. dropna cannot catch missingness that has been written down as a fake number. Remember the Amniote database recoding its -999s? Same disease, different symptom — and the only cure is looking at the chart.

5 · Amniote: a table where the groupby already happened

The Amniote life-history database is one row per species — 21,322 of them across birds, mammals and reptiles. Somebody else already did the aggregation; what you download is the equivalent of species_means above. (In the published archive, missing values are coded -999; the copy served here has them recoded as proper missing values.)

Broad allometry: how does maximum longevity scale with adult body mass, from a 2-gram shrew to a multi-tonne crocodile? Note the cleaning step is the same decision as before — and it costs far more rows here:

Only 5,122 of 21,322 species — 24% — have both traits recorded. Missingness is not decoration in comparative databases; it is most of the table.

Your turn: one rank up

Repeat the aggregation one taxonomic rank up: collapse species into family means and redraw the mass–longevity plot. Average the logged values (a family containing a 3 kg and a 3,000 kg species should average in ratio space, not gram space).

Hint 1 — Group by two keys — class and family — so each family keeps its class for colouring, and remember .reset_index() afterwards so class becomes an ordinary column again.

Hint 2 — Average the two logged columns in the groupby, then reuse the plotting pattern from the species-level chart. You should end up with 327 points.

How to know you got it. This exercise has no solution button — the chart is yours. Check yourself against three facts: you should have 327 families; the correlation should come out around 0.65, up from 0.52 at species level; and the long-lived eccentrics (naked mole-rats, bats, seabirds) should have vanished into their families’ averages. Then ask yourself why the instructions insisted on averaging the logged values — what would one elephant have done to its family in raw grams?

What aggregation did, and did not, do

The relationship was “the same” at every level — log-linear, positive — but every property a reader can quote changed with the level: n went from 90,371 measurements to 69,509 usable ones, to 10,601 species, to 327 families; the correlation moved; outliers appeared and vanished. A claim made from one level does not automatically hold at another. When you caption a chart, the level of aggregation is part of the claim — say what one point is, say what n is, and say where the dropped rows went.
