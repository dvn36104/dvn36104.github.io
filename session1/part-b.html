The Tree of Life

In Part A you worked with a table of amniotes: one row per species, no structure between the rows. But those species are not an unordered bag: every one of them sits on a family tree that connects it, through shared ancestors, to every other living thing. This part is about trees: how they are stored as data, how to draw them (rectangular, round, and against a time axis), and what the words biologists use for them (clade, ancestor, divergence) mean on the screen. We start with a tree you already have: the one hiding in Part A’s own taxonomy columns.

All code runs in your browser. The first cell may take a few seconds while Python loads.

1 · A tree from Part A’s columns

Part A treated every dataset as a flat table: rows and columns, with no structure between the rows. But some data is hierarchical: each item belongs to exactly one group, each group to exactly one larger group, and so on up to a single root. File systems are hierarchical (files inside folders inside folders); so are org charts, postal addresses, and the taxonomy columns of Part A’s amniote table, where every species sits in a genus, every genus in a family, every family in an order, and every order in a class. A hierarchy is exactly the structure a tree draws: one parent per item, one root, no cycles. Part A’s table therefore already contains a tree. Before touching any new data, build it.

First the machinery: a node structure, a layout that gives every node two coordinates (x is distance from the root; y spreads the tips one per row, with each ancestor centred on its children), and a drawing function. One trick worth noting: instead of one ax.plot call per branch, thousands of tiny artists, it collects all segments of a colour into a single line broken by None gaps and draws each colour once, which is orders of magnitude faster.

This is a real tree, and it is honest about what the table knows. But look at what it cannot say. Every internal node is a wide polytomy, a many-way split: Aves fans straight out into 40 orders because the columns record membership, not history. The x axis is rank, not time. The vertical order of siblings is arbitrary. And nothing in it makes one order closer to another: passerines are as related to parrots as to ostriches, as far as these columns know. To go further we need data built to store resolved, dated relationships.

That is what the rest of this page uses. Note what changed and why: the amniote table stores traits and rank membership; the files below store what the table cannot, resolved relationships with dates, and not a single measurement. Same animals, complementary data structures, different sources. All three come from TimeTree (Kumar et al. 2022), and one of them is the family tree of exactly the amniotes whose traits you analysed in Part A.

2 · Reading a phylogenetic tree

A phylogenetic tree has three kinds of parts, and each one asserts something:

  • Tips (leaves) are observed things: living species, families, classes. They are the only part anyone has ever seen.
  • Internal nodes are inferred ancestors: “these two lineages were once one population.” Nobody has seen one; each is a hypothesis.
  • Branch lengths, in the trees on this page, are time: a branch of length 6.7 means 6.7 million years of independent evolution.

Where do the trees come from? Mostly from genetics. Take the same gene from many species, line the DNA sequences up, and count differences: mutations accumulate roughly steadily, so more differences means more time since two lineages separated. That gives the shape and relative ages. To turn relative ages into millions of years, the tree is calibrated against fossils whose age and position are known. TimeTree, the source of our files, aggregates thousands of such published studies into consensus dated trees, which is why every tree here comes with real ages attached.

3 · The Newick format

Before we can draw a tree we need it as data. The standard format, Newick, writes a tree as nested parentheses: each pair of parentheses is one internal node (one ancestor), and :number after a name is the branch length. Here is one you can read by eye: humans, chimps and gorillas, with TimeTree’s ages in millions of years (Myr):

((Homo:6.7, Pan:6.7):2.2, Gorilla:8.9);

Read from the inside out: Homo and Pan each sit at the end of a 6.7-Myr branch from their shared ancestor, so the human–chimp split was 6.7 million years ago. That ancestor connects, 2.2 Myr further back, to the ancestor it shares with Gorilla: 6.7 + 2.2 = 8.9 Myr, exactly Gorilla’s own branch length. In a dated tree every tip is the same total distance from the root, because every living lineage has had the same amount of time.

5 · Clades and common ancestors

A clade is an ancestor plus all of its descendants: a complete subtree, snapped off at one internal node. “Mammals” is a clade; “cold-blooded animals” is not, because their common ancestor also has warm-blooded descendants. Clades are the only groups a tree can actually point at, and the way to find one is through the MRCA, the most recent common ancestor of some tips you name:

Name two primate families and you get all of Primates, every family descended from the same ancestor, ~74 Myr ago:

Look at the tips before moving on: Hominidae (all great apes, including you) is one twig among nineteen. And Paleopropithecidae, the sloth lemurs, are extinct: humans reached Madagascar roughly two thousand years ago, and the last sloth lemurs disappeared around 500 years ago. A dated tree can carry recently-lost lineages alongside living ones, though nothing in the drawing itself marks them as gone.

Taxonomy columns versus a phylogeny

Section 1 built the tree that Part A’s columns encode. Now that we have a resolved, dated clade, put the two side by side. Take every primate family from the life-history table and hang it off one node, that tree’s primate slice, and draw it next to the TimeTree primate clade:

The left tree is real, but look at what it knows. Every family attaches directly to the order: one giant polytomy, because the table records membership, not relatedness, and its branch lengths mean nothing. The right tree makes claims the left cannot: apes split from monkeys before either split from lemurs, and every split has a date. Rank nesting gives you a tree of names; a phylogeny gives you a tree of events.

Even the family lists disagree between the two sources: a one-letter spelling drift and an extinct lineage. Whenever two databases must be joined on names, this is the normal condition, not the exception.

Your turn: the carnivore clade

Cats and dogs are both carnivorans. Extract the clade containing Canidae (dogs) and Felidae (cats), draw it with labels, and print how many families it holds and how long ago their common ancestor lived.

Hint 1: This is the primate example with two different anchor names: clade(amniotes, ["Canidae", "Felidae"]).

Hint 2: Draw with draw_tree(carnivora, ax, labels=True, fontsize=9). You should see 16 families and an ancestor ~55 Myr back.

Fully worked solution:

carnivora = clade(amniotes, ["Canidae", "Felidae"])

print(f"{len(carnivora.leaves())} families, common ancestor "
      f"{root_age - carnivora.depth:.1f} Myr ago")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
draw_tree(carnivora, ax, labels=True, fontsize=9)
ax.set_xlabel("Myr since the amniote root")
plt.show()

16 families from one ancestor ~55 Myr ago, and the tree answers questions the table never could. Cats’ closest relatives here are the civets, hyaenas and mongooses, not dogs; dogs sit on the other side of the very first split, nearer to bears and seals. “Cats and dogs” feel like a natural pair to us, but the clade says they have been separate lineages almost since the dinosaurs left.

6 · The round tree of life

Rectangular layout runs out of room fast: tips need vertical space, and at hundreds of tips the picture becomes a wall. The classic fix is to bend the y-axis into a circle: angle becomes tip position, radius becomes time. Same coordinates, different projection:

We colour three clades (animals, fungi, green plants) by naming two anchor tips for each, exactly as we did for primates. Everything not in a painted clade stays grey:

Three things this picture says that a list of species cannot:

  • Everything familiar is a wedge. All animals (sponges to humans) are one blue sector. All of Mammalia is a single tick mark.
  • The grey is not filler. A third of the disc is protists: algae, amoebae, ciliates, parasites. “Protist” is not a clade; it is the word for every eukaryote outside the three painted wedges, and the tree shows they do not form one group.
  • Fungi sit next to animals, not plants. The red and blue wedges share an ancestor long after either shared one with plants: mushrooms are, genealogically, closer to you than to anything green.

The eukaryote class tree is a completely different file, built from a different set of studies at a different taxonomic rank, but it contains Mammalia and Aves as tips. Write a divergence(root, a, b) helper and use it to date the mammal–bird split in this tree, then compare.

Hint 1: The split date of two tips is the age of their most recent common ancestor, and you already have a helper on this page that finds that node from two names. What it stores is measured from the root, and you want years before today.

Hint 2: In a dated tree, every living tip is the same total distance from the root, so the root’s age is the depth of any tip (safest: the maximum leaf depth). Age and depth run in opposite directions.

How to know you got it. No solution button here: this function is yours. If it works, the two printed dates agree exactly. Before you celebrate independent confirmation, ask two questions. First, why is the mammal–bird split the very same event as the amniote root? Second, both numbers ultimately came from the same database: what would it actually take for two dates like these to count as independent evidence?

What a tree shows and what it does not

Reading a phylogeny is a visual skill worth being pedantic about:

  • Only the nodes and branch lengths carry information. The vertical order of tips is arbitrary: every internal node can be flipped like a mobile, so “top of the chart” means nothing. Humans are not “at the end” of the tree; every living tip is exactly the same distance from the root.
  • The level of aggregation is part of the claim: the Part A lesson again. At phylum level no lineage dies in the great extinctions; at family level, recently lost lineages like the sloth lemurs sit in the tree beside the living. Same history, different rank, different story.
  • Every internal node is an inference, and some are genuinely contested: whether sponges or comb jellies split from the rest of the animals first is a live argument in the literature, and this page’s files quietly take a side. A drawn tree always looks more certain than the science behind it.
